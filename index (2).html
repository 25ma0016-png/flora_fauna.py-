Abstract

This project studies how nature is named in a small corpus of five texts. A vocabulary of 291 unique plant and animal words was collected: 93 flora tokens and 198 fauna tokens. The words were grouped into thematic categories and examined through counts, word-length distribution and the share of flora against fauna. The study shows how simple computational counting can open up questions about how texts describe the natural world, while also showing where such counting stops being useful.

1. Objective

  • To collect the flora and fauna vocabulary found across the five texts.
  • To group the vocabulary into meaningful categories such as trees, crops, birds and aquatic life.
  • To visualise the balance between plant and animal words using a bar graph, a histogram and a pie chart.
  • To reflect on what a vocabulary-level reading can and cannot tell us about the texts.

2. Research question

Main question: How are flora and fauna represented in the vocabulary of the five texts, and what does the balance between the two suggest about how nature is described?

Sub-questions: Which categories of plants and animals appear most often in the word list? Are the words mostly short and everyday, or long and specialised?

3. Methodology

The study follows a distant-reading approach. Words that name plants or animals were identified across the five texts and stored as two Python lists, flora_tokens and fauna_tokens, in the file flora_fauna.py. Singular and plural forms were kept as separate tokens, so counts reflect word forms, not species.

The lists were then merged into one sorted set of unique tokens. Each token was assigned to a category using a manual grouping (for example, tiger to mammals, hilsa to fish and aquatic life, jasmine to flowers). Charts on this page are drawn from the lists in the browser.

6. Discussion

The word list shows that the texts draw on a wide natural vocabulary. Fauna words outnumber flora words, and the fauna list covers mammals, birds, reptiles, insects and a large group of fish and sea creatures. Flora words lean towards crops and farming terms, such as paddy, millet and harvest, next to wild trees and flowers.

The histogram shows that most tokens are short, between three and eight letters. This suggests everyday, familiar naming, not technical or scientific terms. A mix of farming words and wild-nature words points to nature being present as both a working landscape and a wild setting. These are readings of a vocabulary list. They are suggestions to check against the texts, not conclusions about them.

7. Limitations

  • The study counts vocabulary, not how often each word occurs, so it cannot say which words dominate a text.
  • Singular and plural forms are counted separately, which inflates totals.
  • Words with several meanings (sea, cricket, field, crane, bark) may not always refer to nature in context.
  • Categories were assigned by hand and are open to other choices.
  • The list does not show which token belongs to which of the five texts, so comparison between texts is not possible yet.

8. Future work

  • Count word frequency in each text and compare the texts with each other.
  • Lemmatise tokens so singular and plural forms merge.
  • Add context analysis (words around each token) to separate literal from figurative use.
  • Map flora and fauna words to regions or settings named in the texts.
  • Build an interactive word-cloud or network view.
